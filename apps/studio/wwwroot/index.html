Mastering

Pick up where you left off

Move your mix toward a song you love.

Give it your song and a song you wish yours sounded like. Both get split into their separate instruments, then your bass is compared with theirs, your drums with theirs, and so on — and you get told what that song does differently, one difference at a time, with a control for each.

  1. Your song and, if you have one, a reference.
  2. Both are split into vocals, drums, bass, guitar, piano and the rest. This is the slow part — roughly the length of each song, once.
  3. Instrument by instrument, you see what differs and try the suggestions you like. Nothing is applied until you say so, and every one can be undone.
  4. The drums go one level further if you want them to — your kick against their kick, and the snare, cymbals and toms separately.
  5. Then the whole mix is compared for the finishing moves, and you export.

How your mix compares

Comparing instrument by instrument — width, placement and how wet each part sounds. This reads every stem on both sides, so it takes a moment.

Instrument by instrument

Your bass against that song's bass, your drums against its drums — level, tone in five bands, how evenly each part plays, where it sits and how wide it is. Each difference gets its own control, so you can take the air on the drums and leave everything else alone. The drums then go one level further, into your kick against their kick, and the snare, cymbals and toms separately.

Moving these is audible straight away — no re-render. The monitor runs the same band gains as the export, but its filters and compressor are the browser's, so it is a close preview rather than the finished master. Press Master for the real thing.

Why these settings?

This does not change the MP3. MP3 has a bitrate, not a bit depth — the encoder takes 16-bit and there is nothing on that path to choose. A deeper WAV is worth having when the master is going back into another tool, and makes no audible difference to a finished file.

What these do The short version of why each control exists.

Brightness is a shelf on top of the matched tone — the match can only give you the reference's top end, not more of it. Width spreads everything above 250 Hz and leaves the bass centered, so it survives a mono system. Extra headroom sits further under the reference; the master already stops short of over-driving its own limiter.

How the tone lines up

 

What that record is actually doing

One level below the instrument comparison. The mastering page tells you what to change about your mix; this tells you how that record was played and how its kit is put together — where every drum sits across the bar, and your kick against their kick rather than your drums against their drums.

What each record plays

Where the kick, the snare and the hats fall across the bar, how far from the grid they sit, the swing, and whether the bass gets out of the kick's way. Nothing here has a dial behind it — where a record's hats sit against the beat is a fact about a performance, and moving yours there would be composition rather than mastering.

Play a part on something else

Reads the notes out of one stem and plays them on a synth. Measured through this app's own separation, every note of a part comes back and the pitches are as accurate as a clean DI — what separation adds is extra notes, by breaking held ones into pieces, and those are put back together before anything is played. Nothing is quantized or re-timed unless you choose to: the notes land where you played them, and Edit notes is there for when you want them somewhere else.

128 BPM, dance drums, an 808 bass, plucked synths for the guitars, the pump, and a four-on-the-floor kick — then change anything you like below.

Sounds wrong? Tempo detectors often land on half, double or two-thirds of the real tempo — tap whichever matches what you hear:

Editing as played

Click empty space to add a note. Drag a note to move it, its right edge to make it longer. Shift-click to pick several; Delete removes, the arrow keys move and transpose, Ctrl+Z undoes, double-click deletes one. In the velocity lane, drag a stick to make a note harder or softer - the selected ones move together - or drag across empty space to draw, say, a crescendo. Alt+Up and Alt+Down do the same from the keyboard.

Sample a sound from your reference

Not loving the synths? Take the sound straight from your reference. Pick one of its parts and this finds its clean notes - one pitch at a time, held steady - and plays each the way it would be sampled: cut at its attack, tuned to the note, held. Tick the ones to keep and they become an instrument you can put on any part. More notes at different pitches sound more natural across a part.

    Use a sample of your own

    Any short one-shot — a kick, a stab, a vocal chop. If it has a clear pitch it gets transposed to follow the part; if it does not, like a snare, it gets triggered at its own pitch instead. Only upload audio you own or are licensed to use. It stays on this machine.

    Make a kit from your one-shots

    Upload hits above, and they are sorted into kick, snare and hat by how they sound. Pick one for each drum - any left empty plays the edm kit's.

    Shape a sound

    Start from any voice or kit and turn the knobs - each one is named for what you will hear. Every change plays the new sound. Double-click a knob to put it back.

    Your sounds

    Click a step to play it, again to play it softly, again to rest. It keeps the feel of the beat it started from on every step that beat already played.

    Arrangement

    Hear a section, add it, move it, play it twice, mute parts in one block - a breakdown is the chorus without the drums - and drop into any block you like. Cuts land on the bar lines, so the beat never stumbles.

    The song's sections

    Every bar of the song, as tall as it is loud - a chorus usually stands taller. Click a bar to start a part there, name it, and press play to check it starts where you hear it. Parts with the same name are treated as the same part.
